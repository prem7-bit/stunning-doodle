    Three ways to run

    Who does the winding, and who does the counting.

    Hand-wound

    You turn the crown, usually once a day. The simplest mechanical layout, so the movement can be very thin.

    Reserve: about 38 to 80 hours

    Automatic

    A weighted rotor swings with your wrist and winds the spring for you. Leave it off for a couple of days and it stops.

    Rotor wound by wrist motion

    Quartz

    A battery sends current through a small quartz crystal that vibrates at a fixed rate. A circuit counts it and steps the hands once a second.

    Crystal: 32,768 Hz

    Spec sheet

    Seven numbers worth reading.

    Case diameter
    Width of the case without the crown. Most everyday and dress watches fall between 36 and 42 mm.
    mm
    Lug-to-lug
    Distance from the tip of the top lugs to the tip of the bottom lugs. It decides whether a watch sits on your wrist or hangs over the edge.
    mm
    Lug width
    Space between the lugs, which is the strap width you can fit. 18, 20 and 22 mm are common.
    mm
    Water resistance
    A pressure test rating, not a depth you should dive to. 30 m means splashes. 100 m is fine for swimming. ISO 6425 covers dive watches.
    m, or ATM (1 ATM is about 10 m)
    Power reserve
    How long a fully wound mechanical watch keeps running with no further winding.
    hours
    Beat rate
    Half-swings of the balance in one hour. Higher rates tend to steady the watch against knocks, and wear the oil faster.
    bph, or Hz
    Accuracy
    COSC chronometer certification allows a mechanical movement between minus 4 and plus 6 seconds a day.
    seconds per day

    A short timeline

    From a pocket spring to a computer on the wrist.

    1. c. 1510

      Portable clocks in Nuremberg

      Peter Henlein is traditionally credited with small spring-driven timekeepers worn on the body. Historians still argue over the details.

    2. 1675

      The balance spring

      Christiaan Huygens adds a spiral spring to the balance wheel. Pocket watches become far steadier.

    3. 1761

      Harrison's H4 goes to sea

      John Harrison's marine chronometer is tested on a voyage to Jamaica and keeps time well enough to find longitude.

    4. 1926

      The Oyster case

      Rolex launches a wristwatch case sealed with a screw-down crown, and sells the idea of a watch you can get wet.

    5. 1969

      Seiko Astron

      The first quartz wristwatch goes on sale in Tokyo on Christmas Day. Mechanical watchmaking has to find a new reason to exist.

    6. 2015

      Apple Watch

      A watch that runs apps ships in April. The mechanical beat carries on beside it.
